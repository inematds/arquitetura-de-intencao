🪜 "The Prompt Isn’t Enough": The Ceiling of Better Asking

Imagine that every time you want coffee, you have to explain from scratch to the barista what coffee is, how you like it, which cup to use, and that it can’t be cold. It works—sometimes. But the result depends on the barista’s mood, how much you remembered to explain, and sheer luck. That’s exactly how a standalone prompt works: every request starts from scratch, with no memory, no rules, and no criteria for success. Before learning the theory, fix this mental model: a prompt is a one-off request; architecture is the system that makes the request reliable.

A prompt isn’t enough. This is the first signature line of this course, and you’ll hear it again. A prompt is a single instruction, written in the heat of the moment, that the AI interprets without any structure behind it. When it works, it feels like magic. When it fails, you don’t know why — so you try again, changing the words and hoping to get it right.

That’s the the limit of “asking better”: you’re optimizing for luck. Vague intent produces fragile results. If you ask, “write a good email to my customer,” the AI doesn’t know who the customer is, what your brand’s tone is, what’s already been discussed, or what you consider “good.” It improvises—and improvisation is inherently unreliable.

Poor context forces the AI to fill in gaps on its own. And it fills them with the internet average, not your reality. That’s why the same prompt delivers great results one time and mediocre ones the next: what changed was the interpretation, not your request.

The problem isn’t that prompts are bad. Prompts are great — for one-off, exploratory, disposable tasks. The problem is depending on them for something that needs to be reliable, repeatable, and aligned. Trial and error doesn’t scale. You can’t train a team, run a process, or trust a result that changes every time.

The shift begins when you stop asking “what magic words should I use?” and start asking “what information, rule, and criteria does the AI need to get it right every time?” That’s the difference between pressing a button and building a machine.

A small business needs to follow up with a late-paying customer without damaging the relationship.

✗ Fragile

“Write an email asking a late-paying customer to pay.”

✓ Structured

“Write a payment reminder email. Context: a repeat customer for 2 years, an R$1.800 invoice that is 8 days overdue, and this is their first late payment. Our brand tone: friendly, direct, no threats. Goal: receive payment within 5 days while maintaining the relationship. Rule: never mention interest in this first message; offer a payment link and a channel for questions. Success = the customer replies or pays without feeling harassed.”

Why it changes: The bad version leaves everything for the AI to guess: who the customer is, the tone, the limit, and what success looks like. The good version carries context (who/how much/how long), a rule (no interest right now), an objective (collect payment in 5 days), and a success criterion (preserve the relationship). The result no longer depends on luck.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Load concrete context: who, what, for how long, what has already happened
  • ✓State explicitly what "good" means — the success criterion
  • ✓State at least one rule or limit (what NOT to do)
  • ✓Treat one-off prompts as drafts, not as a process

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Asking for something "good" or "professional" without defining what that means
  • ✗Assume that AI knows your customer, your brand, or your history
  • ✗Changing words in the dark and hoping to get it right through trial and error
  • ✗Trusting an unstable prompt for a task that needs to be repeated

💡Practical tip

Before sending any important prompt, do the “what if I weren’t here?” test. If someone else read only your prompt, without knowing you, could they judge whether the result was correct? If not, context, an objective, or a criterion is missing — and the AI is in the same position as that person.

Key concepts

Prompt = one-off request

Each prompt starts from scratch. No memory of what came before, no fixed rules, no success criteria. The AI interprets on the fly — and interpretation varies.

Vague intention = fragile result

If you don't define what "good" means, the AI will define it for you — based on the internet average. Vague in, vague out. The result breaks at the first variation.

Poor context = improvisation

Without knowing who the client is, what the tone should be, and what has already happened, the AI fills the gaps by guessing. Improvisation is inherently unstable.

The ceiling of asking better

More effort in the prompt helps a little—until it stops. You’re optimizing luck, not the system. Trial and error doesn’t become a reliable process.

📊 The AI Ladder: 4 Levels

Picture a staircase with four steps. On the bottom step, you do everything with your own hands. With each step up, you hand more execution over to the machine and take on more direction. At the top, you don’t operate anything—you design the system that lets the machine operate well on its own. This staircase isn’t about knowing how to code. It’s about how much you delegate and how much you structure. Keep the image in mind: the higher you go, the less you execute and the more you architect.

The AI ladder has four rungs. Each describes a different way of working with artificial intelligence—and a different role for you.

Step 1 — Coding. The human writes every line and controls all the logic. It's manual work: you decide every step, type every instruction, and take full responsibility for the result. A concrete example: an analyst building a spreadsheet one formula at a time, or a programmer writing code by hand, line by line. Total control, total effort.

Step 2 — Vibe Coding. The human describes the intent in natural language, and the AI generates the first solution. You move from "how to do it" to "what I want." For example: "create a landing page with a name and email form" — and the AI delivers a working draft. You described the vibe; the machine made it real. It's fast, but it's still a one-off request: what you get depends on how well you described it.

Step 3 — Agentic Engineering. The AI works in cycles: it writes, tests, finds errors, corrects them, and delivers. The human stops operating each step and starts DIRECTING. Example: you define the task—“create this report, validate the numbers against the database, and correct anything inconsistent”—and the agent runs the cycle on its own, going back when it makes a mistake. You don’t type every step; you set the objective and supervise the result.

Step 4 — Intent Architecture. Here the focus shifts away from the agent and toward THE SYSTEM THAT GUIDES THE AGENT. You’re no longer assigning a task or directing a cycle: you’re building the context, rules, memory, objectives, and validation that make any agent deliver reliable, repeatable, aligned results — every time. Example: instead of asking for a customer service email, you define the customer service assistant’s system (what it knows, what it can and can’t do, what success looks like, how to validate it) — and it responds to hundreds of customers in the right way.

Notice the shift: at each level, the machine does more and you structure more. This isn't giving up control—it's trading execution control for direction control. And level 4 isn't 'advanced AI'; it's a way of thinking you can apply without writing a single line of code.

The AI ladder—each rung elevates the role of the person guiding it.

Task: answer customer questions about delivery times.

✗ Fragile

Steps 1/2: you write every reply yourself (Coding) or ask the AI “reply to this customer about the timeline” from scratch every time (Vibe Coding). It works in specific cases, but it doesn’t scale and varies with each reply.

✓ Structured

Steps 3/4: you direct an agent that checks the timeline database, drafts the reply, and validates it before sending (Agentic Engineering) — and, at the next level, define the assistant’s system: what it knows about your products, the brand voice, what it CANNOT promise, and how to check whether the reply is correct (Intent Architecture). Now a hundred customers get the right standard.

Why it changes: The task is the same; what changes is who carries the structure. On the lower rungs, the structure is in your head and gets lost with every request. On the higher rungs, the structure becomes a system—and the result stays reliable, repeatable, and aligned without depending on you to repeat everything.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Identify which step you’re at today for each of your tasks
  • ✓Understand that moving up a level means delegating execution, not losing control
  • ✓Recognize that level 4 is a way of thinking, not a technical prerequisite
  • ✓Look for repetitive tasks — they’re the best candidates for moving up the ladder

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Think that a “higher rung” is always better — a one-off task fits well on rung 2
  • ✗Confusing Intent Architecture with “knowing how to code”
  • ✗Jump straight to automation without first structuring context and rules
  • ✗Treat the ladder as a hierarchy of personal value instead of modes of work

💡Practical tip

You’re not on just one rung — you’re on different rungs for different tasks. You might direct agents in marketing (rung 3) and still write every sales email by hand (rung 1). Self-diagnosis is by task, not by person. Map your tasks, not yourself.

Key concepts

Step 1 — Coding

The human writes every line and controls all the logic. Maximum control, maximum effort. Example: building a spreadsheet one formula at a time.

Step 2 — Vibe Coding

The human describes the intent in natural language; the AI generates the 1st solution. You move from "how" to "what." It's still a one-off request.

Step 3 — Agentic Engineering

The AI works in cycles (write, test, correct, deliver). The human stops operating individual steps and starts DIRECTING the process.

Step 4 — Intent Architecture

The focus shifts from the agent to the SYSTEM that guides the agent: context, rules, memory, objectives, and validation. A reliable, repeatable result.

🧗 What Changes at Each Level

At each rung of the ladder, something goes up and something goes down. What goes down is how much you do hands-on. What goes up is the level of your involvement: you go from handling the pieces to designing the factory. Before the theory, picture the promotion: you start as a worker, become a manager, then a director, and finally an architect. The work doesn’t disappear — it moves up a level.

At each step up, the human’s role rises to a higher level. It’s not that you work less—it’s that you work at a higher level of abstraction and impact.

At level 1 (Coding), you WRITE every line. Your attention is on the details of execution: the right formula, the right comma, the right step. If something goes wrong, it’s because you made a mistake. The result is only as good as your hands.

At level 2 (Vibe Coding), you DESCRIBES the intent. You move away from the details and focus on the request: "I want this, more or less like this." AI handles the first execution. Your focus shifts from "how do I type this" to "how do I describe what I want." Quality now depends on the clarity of your request, not your typing.

At level 3 (Agentic Engineering), you DIRECTS the process. It doesn't describe a single request—it defines an objective and lets the agent run the cycle of trying, testing, and correcting. Your attention shifts back to “what's the mission, what are the limits, and how do I know it went well?” You become the director: you set the scene; you don't act in it.

At level 4 (Intent Architecture), you ARCHITECT the system that guides the agent. You don't direct a specific run—you design the conditions so every run goes right: the context the AI carries, the rules that constrain it, the memory it builds, the objectives that guide it, and the validation that confirms it's correct. You become the architect: you don't build the house; you design the blueprint that ensures every house meets the standard.

The progression is clear: execute → describe → direct → architect. Each step frees you from an operational burden and gives you a thinking burden. Climbing the ladder doesn’t make you lazy—it makes you a strategist. That’s why the skill of the future isn’t “using AI”: it’s architecting the intent that gets AI to deliver.

Moving up in four verbs

1

Execute

You write every line. You focus on the details of execution. The result is only as good as your own work — and every step’s mistakes are yours, too.

2

Describe

You describe the intention in natural language. Quality now depends on how clear the request is, not on typing. AI does the first run.

3

Direct

You define the objective and limits; the agent runs the try-test-correct cycle. You become the director: you set the scene, you don’t act in it.

4

Architect

You design the system (context, rules, memory, objectives, validation) that makes every run come out right. You draw the blueprint, not the house.

A creator needs to publish weekly posts in the brand’s voice.

✗ Fragile

Steps 1–2 (execute/describe): you write every post from scratch, or ask “write a post about X” every week. The tone varies, you review everything by hand, and nothing you’ve learned about what works is recorded for next time.

✓ Structured

Steps 3–4 (direct/design): you define the editorial system — who the audience is, the brand voice (with examples of what feels “like us” and what doesn’t), allowed topics, what to avoid, and how to validate a post before publishing. The agent generates it, you direct the adjustments, and the system remembers the patterns that worked. The tone stays consistent week after week.

Why it changes: At the lower level, brand knowledge lives in your head and gets used up with every post. At the higher level, that knowledge becomes a system — persistent memory and rules. You've moved from "the person who writes" to "the person who designs how everything gets written." The result is repeatable without depending on you to redo the brief every time.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Notice which verb describes how you spend your day: executing, describing, directing, or architecting
  • ✓Consciously aim to move up the verb ladder for tasks that repeat
  • ✓Turn what’s “in your head” into explicit context, rules, and memory
  • ✓Accept that moving up a rung trades operational workload for thinking workload

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Getting stuck "executing" tasks that could be directed or architected
  • ✗Direct agents without first defining clear objectives and limits
  • ✗Think that architecting is less work — it’s a different kind of work
  • ✗Keep critical knowledge only in your memory, without recording it in the system

💡Practical tip

Look at a task you redo every week and identify the verb. If you’re still “executing” or “describing” it from scratch every time, it’s the top candidate for moving up a level. The question is: what do I need to record from my own thinking as context, rules, and memory so I never have to explain this again?

🔑 Improvising × Designing: The Turning Point

There’s a huge difference between a musician improvising in a bar and an architect designing a building. Improvisation is quick, creative, and depends on the talent of the moment — but you don’t live in an improvisation. A design is structured, repeatable, and reliable — you trust it with your life. It’s the same with AI. Improvising prompts is playing in the bar. Architecting intent is designing the building. Before the theory, remember: the difference between using AI superficially and building a system is the same as the difference between improvising and designing.

This is the turning point of the entire course. The difference between using AI superficially and building a system is the same as the difference between improvising and designing.

Improvise is the one-off prompt mindset. You react to the moment, try something, see what comes out, and adjust in the dark. It’s flexible and fast—but unstable. Each result is a one-time event you can’t guarantee will happen again. Improvisation shines during exploration and fails in operation.

Design is the architecture mindset. You anticipate what the AI needs to know, what success looks like, what the limits are, and how to validate—all before you ask for anything. It takes more work upfront, but the result becomes reliable, repeatable, and aligned. You don’t hope it works; you build it to work.

The shift happens in the question you ask. Someone improvising asks: “How do I make a better prompt?” It’s an execution question—always looking for the next magic word. Someone designing asks: “What system will guide the AI toward the result I need?” It’s an architecture question—focused on the conditions, not luck.

Notice: the improvisation question keeps you spinning in trial and error forever. The design question gets you out of that cycle. Instead of endlessly improving one isolated request, you build the structure once so every request comes out right.

This doesn’t mean improvising is wrong. Improvising is great for discovering, testing, and prototyping. The mistake is improvising when you needed to design—trusting luck with a task that needed guarantees. Maturity means knowing when to do each. And this course is about adopting a design mindset: stop hunting for the perfect prompt and start designing the right system.

An HR manager wants to use AI to pre-screen candidates for a job opening.

✗ Fragile

Improvise: “Analyze this résumé and tell me if it’s a good fit for the job.” For every résumé, the AI uses a different standard; you don’t know why it accepted or rejected someone, and two similar candidates get opposite judgments. You’re hoping for the best, not screening.

✓ Structured

Design: you define the screening system—the job’s objective criteria (required, preferred, and disqualifying requirements), the rule against considering data that could introduce bias, the response format (score by criterion + rationale), and the validation (manually review every borderline case). Now every résumé goes through the same process, with the reason recorded.

Why it changes: Improvisation produces a judgment that changes with every run and can't be explained — dangerous and unfair in a sensitive process like HR. The project defines criteria, rules, format, and validation upfront — so the result becomes consistent, auditable, and defensible. The question is no longer "is this résumé good?" but "what system decides what good means?"

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Ask "what system guides the AI?" before "what prompt should I use?"
  • ✓Using improvisation to explore and prototype; a project to operate and repeat
  • ✓Invest effort upfront (structure) to gain reliability later
  • ✓Record why a result is correct—not just that it was produced

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Improvise on tasks that need guaranteed, auditable results
  • ✗Hunt endlessly for the "magic word" instead of structuring the system
  • ✗Confusing the speed of improvisation with real productivity
  • ✗Think that designing removes creativity — design is where creativity scales

💡Practical tip

When you catch yourself rewriting the same prompt for the fifth time, stop. That’s a sign you’re improvising something that called for a design. Change the question right away: instead of “how can I improve this request?”, ask “what context, rule, and criterion should I have defined so I never have to rewrite this again?”

Key concepts

Improvise = react to the moment

Try it, see what comes out, adjust in the dark. Fast and flexible, but unstable. Each result is a one-off event you can't guarantee you'll repeat.

Design = anticipate the conditions

Define context, success, limits, and validation before you ask. More work up front means a reliable, repeatable, aligned result later.

The shallow question

“How do I write a better prompt?” — an execution question that traps you in trial and error, always hunting for the next magic word.

The architecture question

“What system guides the AI?” — a design question that gets you out of the loop: build the structure once that makes everything come out right.

⚠️ The 4 pitfalls of shallow use

When an AI result goes wrong, it’s rarely because “AI is stupid.” It’s because a structural piece was missing—and each missing piece causes a specific kind of failure. There are four classic failures, the four breakdowns of shallow use. Learning to recognize them is like learning to read symptoms: you can diagnose exactly what was missing instead of guessing. Remember the chain we’ll use throughout the course—Intention → Context → Process → Result—because each breakdown is a missing piece in that chain.

Shallow AI use collapses in four predictable ways. Each failure is a missing piece of the architecture. Learn to recognize them, and you’ll be able to diagnose any bad result.

Collapse 1 — Intent without structure = noise. You know what you want, but you hand it to AI without organizing it. The request comes out unclear, AI responds in the dark, and the result is noise: lots of text, little value. Example: "help me with my business" — AI returns generalities because the intent arrived without shape. Symptom: generic, off-target answers that work for anyone and no one.

Collapse 2 — Context without an objective = excess. You dump information — documents, history, data — but don’t say what success looks like. AI drowns in the context and doesn’t know what to prioritize. Example: you paste 20 pages of a report and ask, “What do you think?” AI summarizes everything and decides nothing, because context without an objective becomes information overload. Symptom: long, unfocused answers that touch on everything without reaching a conclusion.

Collapse 3 — Automation without rules = risk. You automate a task but don’t define limits. AI acts quickly and at scale — including making mistakes quickly and at scale. Example: a customer support assistant that, without a rule, promises refunds the company doesn’t offer to a thousand customers. Automation without rules becomes a risk. Symptom: errors silently multiply because no one put a safeguard in place.

Collapse 4 — AI without validation = the illusion of productivity. The AI delivers something that looks finished, you trust it, and move on. But no one checked whether it’s correct. You feel like you’ve accomplished a lot—until the error shows up later, at a high cost. Example: a report with fabricated numbers that you only discover in a meeting with the customer. AI without validation creates the illusion of productivity. Symptom: a sense of efficiency today, rework and losses tomorrow.

The chain Intention → Context → Process → Result organizes everything: the intent needs structure, the context needs an objective, the process (automation) needs a rule, and the result needs validation. Remove one piece and you know exactly what will collapse. This is your diagnostic kit for the 4 days.

Without structure, each part of shallow usage collapses in a different way.

An AI assistant handling customer service for a small business started generating complaints.

✗ Fragile

Shallow diagnosis: "AI is bad, I’ll switch tools." You switch, spend time, and the problem comes back — because the tool wasn’t the cause.

✓ Structured

Structural diagnosis: run the case through the 4 collapses. Were the answers generic? (intent without structure). Were they scattered because of too much unfocused data? (context without an objective). Did the assistant promise something prohibited? (automation without rules). Did no one check the answers before sending them? (AI without validation). You discover that the problem was collapse 3: the rule "never promise a deadline under 48h" was missing. You add the rule, and the symptom disappears.

Why it changes: A shallow diagnosis blames the tool and solves nothing. A structural diagnosis uses the 4 breakdowns as a checklist and pinpoints the missing piece. You move from "AI is bad" to "rule X was missing" — a problem you can fix. Diagnosing the missing piece is what separates people who complain from people who fix things.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓When faced with a bad result, run through the 4 collapses as a diagnostic checklist
  • ✓Ask which piece was missing: structure, objective, rule, or validation
  • ✓Match each symptom to its structural cause before changing the tool
  • ✓Using the Intention → Context → Process → Result chain to locate the failure

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Blaming “the AI” in general instead of finding the missing piece
  • ✗Add more context when the problem was a lack of an objective
  • ✗Automate at scale before defining the rules
  • ✗Trusting a result that looks ready without any validation

💡Practical tip

Memorize the 4 failure modes as four diagnostic questions: 1) Was the intent structured? 2) Did the context have a goal? 3) Did the automation have a rule? 4) Was the result validated? Any poor AI result fails at least one of these. Find the missing piece, and you’ve found the fix.

Key concepts

Intention without structure = noise

You know what you want but don’t organize it. AI responds in the dark and gives you generalities. Symptom: answers that could apply to anyone.

Context without an objective = excess

You dump information without saying what success looks like. AI drowns in it and doesn’t prioritize. Symptom: long answers that touch on everything and conclude nothing.

Automation without rules = risk

You automate without limits. AI makes mistakes quickly and at scale. Symptom: errors silently multiply because there was no safeguard.

AI without validation = the illusion of productivity

You trust what looks finished without checking. Symptom: a sense of efficiency today, rework and costly losses tomorrow.

🎯 Where are you? Self-assessment

Every transformation starts with an honest map of where you are. You can't choose the path without knowing your starting point. Today you'll make two moves: locate, task by task, which rung of the ladder you operate on—and choose ONE real intent from your world to develop over the 4 days. This intent will be your lab case: on Day 2 you'll architect it with the 5 pillars, and on Day 3 you'll turn it into a system that can be validated. Choose something that truly matters.

This is the topic-deliverable of the day. Here, theory becomes a personal diagnosis and a concrete starting point.

First step: task-based self-assessment. List 3 to 5 tasks you do with AI today—or would like to do. For each one, identify the level: Do you perform each step yourself (1, Coding)? Describe it and have AI generate the first version (2, Vibe Coding)? Direct an agent that runs cycles (3, Agentic Engineering)? Or architect the system that guides the agent (4, Intent Architecture)? Be honest. Most people are at levels 1 and 2 for most tasks—and that’s fine; this is the real starting point.

Second move: detect the collapses. For tasks where the result is unstable, check the 4 failure modes. Which piece is missing? That already tells you what to design first.

Third move: choose 1 real intent. Of all your tasks, choose ONE to be your 4-day lab case. A good intention has three marks: it’s real (it happens in your work or life), repeatable (you do it more than once, so it’s worth structuring), and important (the result has consequences). Avoid choosing something trivial “to make it easier” — the more real it is, the more you learn.

Examples of good intentions: a customer service assistant for your business; a system that standardizes your branded posts; a workflow that pre-screens résumés; a process that turns meetings into reliable minutes; an agent that answers basic legal questions within safe limits.

Today's deliverable is the Pyramid Position Map filled in: your tasks, your steps, the collapses you identified, and — highlighted — the 1 real intention you’ll carry into day 2. This map is the foundation for everything that follows. Without a clear starting point, there’s no project. With one, you’ve already moved beyond the surface.

Student who owns an online store decides which case to bring as a lab case.

✗ Fragile

“I’m going to ask AI to write a poem about my store.” It’s one-off, doesn’t repeat, and has no real consequences. As a lab exercise, it teaches little — there’s nothing to architect, validate, or repeat.

✓ Structured

“I’m going to build an AI assistant that answers my customers’ frequently asked questions (delivery time, returns, shipping) in my store’s tone, without promising what I can’t deliver.” It’s real (happens every day), repeatable (hundreds of customers), and important (affects sales and reputation).

Why it changes: The bad version is a one-time exercise with no structure to build on. The good version is a living system: it has context (products, policies), objectives (answer the question and make a sale), rules (don’t overpromise), memory (recurring questions), and validation (correct answer before sending). It’s exactly the kind of intent that takes shape over the 4 days.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓List 3–5 real tasks you do or want to do with AI
  • ✓Honestly classify each one on the scale from 1 to 4
  • ✓Run the 4 collapses on unstable tasks to find the missing piece
  • ✓Choose 1 real, repeatable, important intent as your case for the 4 days

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Choose a trivial intent “to make it easier” — you’ll learn less
  • ✗If you classify something at a high level out of vanity instead of honesty
  • ✗Choose something one-off that doesn’t repeat (there’s nothing to architect)
  • ✗Leave day 1 without a completed Map and a defined intention

💡Practical tip

To test whether your intention is a good one, ask three questions: Does it happen again? Does anyone care about the result? Could I explain it to someone else so they could do it? If you answered yes to all three, you have a great lab case. If you answered no to any of them, choose another—the quality of your learning over the next 2 days depends on this choice.

Key concepts

Diagnosis is task-specific

You’re not on a single rung. List 3-5 tasks and classify each one. The real picture emerges task by task, not person by person.

Honesty is a prerequisite

Most people are on rungs 1–2 for most tasks. Admitting that isn’t weakness—it’s the starting point that makes moving up possible.

The 3 marks of good intent

Real (happens in your world), repeatable (you do it more than once), and important (has consequences). The more real it is, the more you learn.

🛠️ Practical Exercises

Use your real case. The answer key is an example, not the only correct answer.

1. Rewrite the fragile prompt

Objective: Experience firsthand the difference between a vague intention and a structured one.

Take a vague, "shallow" prompt you might use day to day (e.g., "write a post for my company"). Rewrite it by adding four things: (1) concrete context, (2) an objective/success criterion, (3) at least one rule or limit, and (4) what a "correct" result would look like. Compare the two in your mind: which one depends less on luck?

  1. Write the original shallow prompt, exactly as you’d send it on impulse
  2. Add context: who, what, for whom, and in what situation
  3. Add an objective: what success looks like and why
  4. Add 1 rule/limit: what the AI must NOT do
  5. Add the “correct” criterion: how you’ll know it turned out well
  6. Reread and mark what changed from unstable to reliable
See the sample answer key
SHALLOW PROMPT: “Write a post to promote my English course.” STRUCTURED PROMPT: “Write an Instagram post to promote my English course. CONTEXT: online course for adults ages 30-45 who studied English in school but got stuck; my brand is encouraging and doesn’t offer ‘magic formulas.’ GOAL: get sign-ups for a free class on Thursday; success = comment ‘I’m in’ or click the link. RULE: don’t promise quick fluency or use clichés like ‘English in 30 days’; maximum 4 lines + 1 call to action. RIGHT = sounds like a conversation with someone who understands the frustration of getting stuck, not an aggressive ad.” DIFFERENCE: the shallow prompt leaves the tone, audience, promise, and format for the AI to guess—and it will use the average internet cliché. The structured prompt includes context, a goal, a rule, and a criterion, so the result no longer depends on luck and instead depends on structure.

💡 Tip: If you get stuck on any of the four, that’s exactly the element your original prompt was missing—and the reason the result varied.

2. Classify 5 tasks on the ladder

Objective: Train yourself to recognize which rung you’re operating on, task by task.

List 5 tasks you do (or want to do) with AI. For each one, mark its current level (1 Coding, 2 Vibe Coding, 3 Agentic Engineering, 4 Intent Architecture) and the level it could reach. Justify each classification in one sentence.

  1. List 5 real tasks from your day-to-day work with AI
  2. For each one, identify the verb: do I execute, describe, direct, or design?
  3. Mark the current level (1 to 4)
  4. Ask: does this task repeat? If so, could it move up a step?
  5. Mark the target level and write why it could move up
See the sample answer key
1) Answer customer questions → CURRENT: 2 (I describe each answer) → TARGET: 4 (design an assistant with context, rules, and validation)—because it repeats hundreds of times and the tone needs to stay consistent. 2) Write a business proposal → CURRENT: 2 → TARGET: 3 (direct an agent that builds the proposal from a template and validates the numbers). 3) Summarize meetings into minutes → CURRENT: 1 (I do it by hand) → TARGET: 4 (a system with a fixed format, rules about what to include, and validation). 4) Create an image for a post → CURRENT: 2 → TARGET: 2 (it’s one-off and creative; it works well at step 2 and doesn’t need to move up). 5) Pre-screen resumes → CURRENT: 1 → TARGET: 4 (fixed criteria, an anti-bias rule, an output format, and human validation for edge cases). READING THE MAP: repetitive tasks with consequences (1, 2, 3, 5) call for moving up; the creative, one-off task (4) can stay where it is.

💡 Tip: Not every task should be escalated. The key question is: does it repeat, and does the result matter? If so, escalation pays off. If it’s one-off and creative, level 2 is enough.

3. Diagnose the collapse

Objective: Apply the 4 collapses as a diagnostic tool in real cases.

For each of the three scenarios below, identify WHICH of the 4 failure modes is happening (intention without structure / context without a goal / automation without rules / AI without validation) and suggest the missing piece. Scenario A: you pasted 15 pages of a contract and asked, “What do you think?”—the AI returned a huge summary without pointing out anything useful. Scenario B: your customer service bot replied to 200 customers offering a discount that doesn’t exist. Scenario C: you gave your boss an AI-generated report and discovered in the meeting that two numbers were wrong.

  1. Read each scenario and identify the symptom
  2. Match the symptom to the 4 failure modes
  3. Name the breakdown in each scenario
  4. Suggest the missing structural component to fix it
See the sample answer key
SCENARIO A → Collapse 2: context without an objective = overload. You gave it lots of material and no objective, so the AI summarized everything and decided nothing. MISSING PIECE: a clear objective — e.g., “list the 3 highest-risk clauses for the client and explain why each one is risky.” SCENARIO B → Collapse 3: automation without rules = risk. The bot acted at scale without limits, and the error multiplied 200-fold. MISSING PIECE: explicit rules — e.g., “never offer discounts; discounts require human approval” + a list of what it can and can’t promise. SCENARIO C → Collapse 4: AI without validation = the illusion of productivity. It looked done, you trusted it, and no one checked. MISSING PIECE: a validation step — e.g., check every number against the source before delivering, or flag unverified data.

💡 Tip: The symptom reveals the failure. “Lots of text, nothing useful” = excess (context without an objective). “Error multiplied at scale” = risk (automation without a rule). “It looked right, but was wrong” = illusion (no validation).

4. Fill in your Position Map on the Pyramid

Objective: Produce the day’s deliverable: position diagnosis + choice of a real intention.

Fill in the Pyramid Position Map template with YOUR real tasks. At the end, choose and highlight 1 real intention you’ll develop over the 4 days. Test the intention against the 3 markers: real, repeatable, matters.

  1. List your 3–5 tasks using the template
  2. Classify each one at its current level and mark the collapses you notice
  3. Define the target level for each task
  4. Choose the 1 real intent among them
  5. Apply the 3 marks test (real? repeatable? matters?)
  6. Write the intent in one clear sentence, as you would take it into day 2
See the sample answer key
TASKS: - Customer service → current rung 2 → perceived collapse: automation without rules (I already tried a bot and it overpromised) → target 4. - Brand posts → current 2 → collapse: intent without structure (tone varies) → target 3/4. - Meeting minutes → current 1 → collapse: AI without validation → target 4. CHOSEN INTENT: "Build an AI assistant that answers my customers' frequently asked questions (lead time, exchanges, shipping) in my store's tone, without promising anything I can't deliver." 3-MARK TEST: Real? Yes, it happens every day. Repeatable? Yes, hundreds of customers/month. Does it matter? Yes, it affects sales and reputation. → Intent approved for the 4 days.

💡 Tip: If your intention meets all 3 criteria, it’s strong. If it fails any—especially “repeatable”—choose another one, or you won’t have anything to architect on day 2.

5. Improvise × Design: classify and justify

Objective: Internalize when to improvise and when design is essential.

For each situation, decide whether the right approach is IMPROVISE (one-off prompt) or DESIGN (system) and justify your answer in one sentence. 1) Quick brainstorm of names for a new product. 2) Automatic support replies sent without human review. 3) Testing a script idea at 11 p.m. out of curiosity. 4) Generating reports that go to the patient. 5) Drafting the outline of an article you’ll rewrite later.

  1. Read each situation
  2. Ask: does the result repeat? Are there consequences? Does it need to be guaranteed?
  3. Choose whether to improvise or design
  4. Justify based on repeatability and consequences
See the sample answer key
1) IMPROVISE — brainstorming is one-off exploration with no consequences; improvising is the right tool. 2) DESIGN — it runs at scale without human review; without rules and validation, it becomes a risk. It requires a system. 3) IMPROVISE — a curiosity test, disposable, with no consequences. 4) DESIGN — high risk with direct consequences for people; it requires context, rules, and rigorous validation. Never improvise. 5) IMPROVISE — it’s a draft you’ll rewrite; you’ll add structure later, yourself. RULE OF THUMB: repeated + consequential = design. One-off + disposable = improvise. The danger lies in improvising when design was needed (cases 2 and 4).

💡 Tip: The costly mistake is never overdesigning—it’s improvising where consequences and scale are involved. When in doubt, look at the consequence of an error: the worse it is, the more you need to design for it.

🎓 To make things easier live (in-person mode)

Total time: 90 min

Activity: Start with the “Fragile Prompt Roulette” (15 min): ask 3 volunteers to dictate, live, a prompt they would actually use; run it onscreen and show how the same prompt produces different results each time — the class sees firsthand the limits of “asking better.” Next, “Climb the Ladder” (25 min): in groups of 3–4, each group gets a task card (customer service, brand content, HR, legal, automation) and classifies which rung it usually sits on and where it could reach; each group presents for 2 min. Then, “Collapse Hunt” (20 min): display 4 real examples of poor results and have the class vote on which of the 4 collapses each one represents, explaining why — the facilitator reveals the answers and leads a discussion. Finally, “My Intention” (30 min): each participant individually fills out the Position on the Pyramid Map and chooses their 1 real intention; in pairs, they apply the 3-mark test to each other and refine the final statement.

Materials: Screen/projector with access to a live AI; printed cards with tasks by domain (customer service, brand, HR, legal, automation); 4 cases of poor results on slides for the Collapse Hunt; printed copies of the Pyramid Position Map template; pens; visible timer; board for posting the chosen intents (they'll be revisited on Day 2).

Discussion questions:

  • ›Which task most often feels like it 'works sometimes and sometimes doesn’t'? What do you think is missing?
  • ›Which of your tasks repeats so often that it clearly calls for moving up a level—and why haven’t you moved up yet?
  • ›Of the 4 collapses, which affects you most day to day: noise, overload, risk, or the illusion of productivity?
  • ›Does your chosen intent pass all 3 marks? If you had to defend it to a skeptic, what would you say?
  • ›Where is improvising the right choice for you—and where has improvising already cost you dearly?

✅Day 1 Summary

  • ✓A prompt isn’t enough: a prompt is a one-off request with no memory or criteria; relying on it for something reliable means relying on luck and trial and error.
  • ✓The AI ladder has 4 rungs—Coding, Vibe Coding, Agentic Engineering, Intention Architecture—and the diagnosis is by task, not by person.
  • ✓At each step up, the human’s role rises: execute → describe → direct → architect. Moving up means trading execution work for thinking work, not losing control.
  • ✓The shift is improvising × designing: the question changes from “How do I make a better prompt?” to “What system guides the AI?”
  • ✓The 4 failure modes of shallow use are your diagnostic kit: intention without structure = noise; context without a goal = overload; automation without rules = risk; AI without validation = an illusion of productivity.
  • ✓You leave day 1 with the Position Map in the Pyramid filled out and 1 real intent (real, repeatable, matters) chosen for the 4 days.
