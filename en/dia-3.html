DAY 3

⚙️ The System: from intention to reliable results

Build and operate: how five completed pillars become a system that delivers reliable, repeatable, aligned results — and survives a tool change.

On days 1 and 2, you mapped your position on the pyramid and designed the 5-pillar Canvas. Today, the Canvas leaves the page and becomes a system. There's only one difference between a polished document and an operating system: the system wheel, é validated e improvement with use. You’ll build it, run it for the first time, validate it against objective criteria, and install the continuous improvement loop. By the end, you’ll have your 1st working Intent Architecture system — not a polished prompt, but an information environment that turns intent into results.

✓Turn the Canvas’s 5 pillars into an end-to-end system, not an isolated prompt.
✓Understand how frameworks, workflows, agents, and memory fit together like gears in the same mechanism.
✓Run the run → validate → adjust → improve cycle as a quality routine, not as a one-time act.
✓Separate the logic (which persists) from the tool (which changes) so you can use any model better than average.
✓Recognize where a system creates real value: as an assistant, process, product, or decision system.
✓Build, run, and validate your 1st complete system, with a documented continuous improvement plan.

🧩 From Pieces to a System

Imagine five engine parts scattered across a workbench: the piston, the spark plug, the crankshaft, the fuel tank, and the speedometer. Each part is perfect, but the car won’t move. The Canvas from day 2 gave you the five right parts—Context, Objectives, Rules, Memory, Validation. Today you assemble them in a way that starts the engine. A system isn’t the sum of its parts: it’s the arrangement that turns intention into results reliably.

One polished prompt is a one-off prompt. You write, the AI responds, and quality depends on your inspiration in that moment. It works once, fails the next time, and no one knows why. That’s improvisation—not a system.

A system is a information environment. Before you ask for anything, the AI already KNOWS who it is, what it needs to deliver, what it can and can’t do, what it learned before, and how the result will be checked. The request becomes the small part; the environment does the heavy lifting.

The key to repeatability is this: what guarantees quality can’t live in your head. If the tone rule, objective, and acceptance criteria only exist when you remember to type them, you are the system—and you can’t scale or sleep. When this becomes a persistent environment, every run, on any day, starts from the same foundation.

The 5 pillars only become a system when they connect into a chain: Intention → Context → Process → Outcome. Context and Memory provide input; Objectives and Rules guide and constrain; Validation closes the loop by telling you whether it worked. Remove one link and the system becomes a prompt that relies on luck again.

The test for whether you have a system is brutally simple: can someone else (or you a month from now) run it and get an equivalent result without you explaining anything live? If so, it’s a system. If it depends on you narrating the context every time, you still have loose pieces.

Repeatable, reliable, and aligned are the three proofs. Repeatable: run it again and get a similar result. Reliable: the result meets the criteria; it doesn’t just look good. Aligned: meets the objective and follows the business rules. A system is what delivers all three at once.

Loose pieces don’t deliver. Put together as a system, they do.

Ready-to-use example — Customer service: loose prompt vs. system

A small business wants to use AI to respond to customers on WhatsApp.

✗ Fragile

Respond to my store's customers in a friendly way and answer their questions.

✓ Structured

CONTEXT: Supplement store, average order value R$ 120, delivery in 5 business days, 7-day exchange policy. OBJECTIVE: resolve the question in the first response whenever possible; success = the customer doesn't need to repeat the question. RULES: never promise a delivery time shorter than 5 days; never offer a discount without approval; if the question is about a refund, forward it to a human. MEMORY: the 10 most frequently asked questions and the approved answers. VALIDATION: every response must cite the correct delivery time, use a courteous tone, and say it will be checked if you don't know — never make anything up.

Why it changes: The loose prompt delivers tone, but doesn’t control deadlines, discounts, or refunds — every response is a gamble. The system version defines what success looks like, what’s forbidden, and how to check, so it runs the same with any customer service rep or on any day. It becomes repeatable, reliable, and aligned.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Write the 5 pillars somewhere persistent that the AI reads on every run (system prompt, context document, knowledge base).
  • ✓Connect the pillars in an explicit chain: what feeds, what guides, what limits, what validates.
  • ✓Apply the third-party test before calling it done.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Treat the day’s request as if it were the system — the request is the tip, not the engine.
  • ✗Keep the rule or objective only in your head and type it from memory every time.
  • ✗Think that a long, polished prompt is already a system; without validation and persistence, it’s still a one-off run.

💡Practical tip

Take the envelope test: write down your system, close the computer, and ask someone else to run it using only what you wrote. Anything they have to ask you about is a pillar that still lives in your head — and needs to become text.

Key concepts

An environment, not a one-off run

A system is the information environment that exists BEFORE the request. The prompt becomes the small part; the environment carries the weight of quality.

Quality comes from your head

Rules, objectives, and criteria need to be written down and persistent. If they exist only when you remember them, you are the system—and you don't scale.

The chain connects the pieces

Intention → Context → Process → Result. The 5 pillars only become a system when they connect in this chain; remove one link and it’s back to luck.

Third-party test

If someone else can run it and get an equivalent result without you explaining it live, it's a system. If it depends on you narrating, the pieces are still loose.

⚙️ The cogs

If the system is an engine, its gears have names. A framework is the engine's design. A workflow is the order in which the parts move. An agent is the part that acts on its own within the workflow. Memory is what the engine remembers from one cycle to the next. You don't need to program any of them to understand how they fit together — and understanding the fit helps you choose better tools.

Framework is the thought structure that organizes the system. The 5 pillars are one framework. The Intention → Context → Process → Result chain is another. A framework isn’t a tool: it’s the mental blueprint that says what needs to exist and in what order. Change the tool, and the framework still holds.

Workflow is the sequence of steps that takes you from intention to result. Example: receive the customer’s message → classify the type of question → find the approved answer in memory → draft a response → validate it against the rules → send it or escalate to a human. The workflow turns a vague request into a production line with named steps.

Agent is the part of the system that runs in cycles without you operating every step: it writes, tests, sees the error, fixes it, and delivers. On day 1, this was layer 3 of the pyramid. Within a system, the agent is a cog that runs part of the workflow autonomously—but always within the rules and validated at the end.

Memory is what persists between runs: approved patterns, previous decisions, brand voice, and errors that must not happen again. Without memory, every day is day one and the AI relearns from scratch. With memory, the system gets better with each cycle because it carries forward what has worked.

O fit is this: the framework defines what exists, the workflow defines the order, the agent runs the autonomous parts, and memory supplies everything with what’s already known. You design the framework and the workflow, decide where an agent fits, and choose what goes into memory. The tools simply fill those roles.

Not every system needs an agent. A content generation system can be a framework + workflow + memory, without any autonomous cycle. Start with the minimum: only add an agent when a step needs to iterate (try, fail, correct) without you in the loop. Complexity is a cost, not a trophy.

Ready-to-use example — Brand content: vague request vs. designed workflow

A creator wants a system that produces weekly posts in her brand’s voice.

✗ Fragile

Write me 5 posts for this week in my style.

✓ Structured

FRAMEWORK: 5 pillars. WORKFLOW: (1) pull the week’s theme and the goal for each post; (2) find the voice guide and 3 high-performing posts in MEMORY; (3) draft the post following the guide; (4) VALIDATE against the checklist (tone, hook in the first 2 seconds, 1 CTA, no banned jargon); (5) mark the ones that pass and return the failures with the reason. AGENT: in steps 3-4, iterate until the draft passes the checklist or, after 2 attempts, escalate to the creator.

Why it changes: The vague request delivers 5 generic texts that you rewrite from scratch. The designed workflow names each step, draws on voice memory, validates against criteria, and uses autonomy only where it makes sense (iterating on the draft). The result arrives validated and on-brand, and improves because the memory keeps what performed well.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Design the workflow as a sequence of named stages before thinking about any tools.
  • ✓Consciously decide what goes into memory — approved patterns and mistakes to avoid repeating.
  • ✓Use an agent only for steps that require iterating without you in the middle.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Confusing a framework (a mental model) with a tool (the app you open).
  • ✗Stacking agents and integrations for status; unnecessary complexity is a cost and a point of failure.
  • ✗Run without memory and complain that AI forgets everything—you didn't give it anything to remember.

💡Practical tip

Draw your workflow in numbered boxes on a sheet of paper before opening any app. For each box, ask: does this need memory? Does this need iteration (an agent)? What rule limits it? That sheet matters more than the choice of tool.

Key concepts

Framework = the mental model

The structure that defines what needs to exist and in what order. The 5 pillars are a framework. It’s independent of any tool—switch tools, and it still stands.

Workflow = the production line

The named sequence of steps from intention to result: receive → classify → search → draft → validate → deliver. It turns a vague request into a process.

Agent = autonomy in a cycle

The mechanism that writes, tests, corrects, and delivers one part of the workflow on its own. Always within the rules and validated at the end. Use it only when the step requires iteration.

Memory = what persists

Patterns, decisions, and mistakes that must not be repeated. Without it, every day is day one. With it, the system improves with every cycle.

3

🔄 The Operating Cycle

A good system isn't born ready — it's born ready to run. The first version always has gaps; what sets real operators apart is having a loop that turns every gap into an improvement. Running, validating, adjusting, and improving isn't a set of steps you do once: it's the routine that keeps the system alive and makes it more reliable over time.

Run is running the system on a real case, not a hypothetical one. Use a real customer message, an actual post topic, or a concrete decision you need to make. A real case exposes gaps that the ideal case hides.

Validate is checking the result against the criteria you defined in the Validation pillar—not against your taste in the moment. Criteria are objective: did it state the correct deadline? follow the discount rule? meet the objective? Validation without written criteria becomes an illusion of productivity: it looks good, but you don’t know if it is.

Adjust is fixing the CAUSE, not the symptom. If the AI promised the wrong deadline, don’t rewrite the response by hand—fix the rule or context that let the error through. A symptom fix has to be redone every time; a cause fix solves it for good.

Improve is raising the system’s ceiling, not just patching holes. Did you notice that the 3 most common questions always get escalated to a human? Add approved answers for them to memory. Continuous improvement is what makes the system in week 10 better than the one in week 1, without starting over.

The loop closes back on itself: each validated run generates a lesson, the lesson becomes a root-cause adjustment or memory improvement, and the next run starts with a higher bar. This is how the system builds quality instead of just repeating.

Two traps kill the loop. The first is don't validate: you run it, like it, move on—and never discover the silent errors. The second is validate without recording: you find the gap, fix it manually, and it comes back the following week because the cause never became a rule. Record what you learned or it will be lost.

A system isn’t delivered ready-made: it runs, validates, adjusts, and improves — always.

The loop, step by step

1

Run with a real case

Run it with a real message, topic, or decision. The ideal case hides gaps; the real case exposes them. A system proves itself by running, not on a slide.

2

Validate against criteria

Check the result against the criteria written in the Validation pillar, not against your mood at the moment. Without objective criteria, validation becomes an illusion.

3

Fix the cause

Recurring errors are fixed in the rule or the context, not by manually rewriting the output. You keep redoing the symptom; you fix the cause once.

4

Improve and rerun

Along with plugging gaps, add what works to memory — and return to step 1 at a higher level. That’s what makes week 10 surpass week 1.

Ready-to-use example — Manual correction vs. root-cause adjustment

The customer service assistant promised delivery in 3 days, but the actual timeframe is 5.

✗ Fragile

The owner sees the error, edits the customer response by changing 3 to 5, and gets on with the day. The following week, the same error happens with another customer.

✓ Structured

The owner logs the gap, identifies the CAUSE (the context didn't include the standard timeframe, so the AI guessed), adds "delivery = 5 business days" to Context, and creates the Rule "never promise a timeframe shorter than the standard one"; they add a "check timeframe" item to Validation. They run the same case again: it passes. The error doesn't come back.

Why it changes: A manual fix solves one customer’s issue and leaves the cause intact—the error is structural and will happen again. A root-cause fix changes the system: it becomes a rule, a validation criterion, and the issue goes away for good. One effort, problem closed, more reliable system.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Always run the first test with a real case from your day, never a perfect example.
  • ✓Validate using the Validation pillar checklist in front of you, item by item.
  • ✓Record every gap in a short log: what failed, the cause, and the adjustment—and re-run the same case to confirm.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Run it, like it, and move on without validating—that's how silent errors survive.
  • ✗Fix the symptom by hand without addressing the cause; the error comes back in a new form.
  • ✗Treat the loop as a one-time launch event, not a weekly routine.

💡Practical tip

Adopt the same-case rule: after fixing the cause, rerun exactly the case that failed. If it passes, you fixed the cause. If it still fails, you fixed a symptom. This is the fastest test for whether the improvement was real.

Key concepts

Run with a real case

Run it with a real message, topic, or decision. The ideal case hides gaps; the real case exposes them. A system proves itself by running, not on a slide.

Validate against criteria, not personal preference

Check the result against the criteria written in the Validation pillar. Without objective criteria, validation becomes an illusion of productivity — it looks good, but you don’t know if it is.

Fix the cause, not the symptom

Recurring errors are fixed in the rule or the context, not by manually rewriting the output. You keep redoing the symptom; you fix the cause once.

Improving raises the ceiling

🧭 Beyond the Tools

Tools change names every quarter. Today's model is replaced by tomorrow's; the app you love disappears or changes its price. People who learned only to click around the tool start over from scratch with every change. People who learned the logic — the 5 pillars, the chain, the loop — carry everything over to the new tool and still use it better than most.

A logic is what doesn’t change: you’ll always need context, an objective, rules, memory, and validation, with any tool and any model. That’s inherent to the problem, not the software. That’s why the Intention Architecture lasts—it operates one layer above the tool.

A tool is just the implementation of the logic. Where you write the context, how you store memory, which button triggers the workflow—that varies. But if you know WHAT needs to exist, figuring out WHERE to put it in a new tool takes minutes, not months.

This changes who’s in control of the relationship. The shallow user is at the mercy of the tool: waits for the tutorial, copies the magic prompt, and gets stuck when the interface changes. The architect approaches a new tool with the system in mind and asks only where to fit each pillar. The tool serves them, not the other way around.

Day 1's focus was this: in Intent Architecture, the focus shifts from the agent to the system that guides the agent. Here, that idea is complete — the focus also shifts from the tool to the logic that guides any tool. It's the same altitude increase.

In practice, document your system independently of any tool: the 5 pillars in plain text, the workflow in numbered boxes, and the validation criteria in a checklist. This document is portable. Migrating from one tool to another becomes a matter of copying and adapting, not starting over.

The portability test is straightforward: if your tool disappeared tomorrow, how much of your system would survive? If the answer is almost everything, you have logic. If it’s almost nothing, all you had was memorized clicks — and is one update away from losing everything.

Ready-to-use example — Tool hostage vs. owner of the logic

The AI tool the team was using ended its free plan; they need to migrate.

✗ Fragile

The team had only a collection of prompts pasted into that specific tool and a memorized click workflow. During the migration, they lost the context, started testing from scratch, and spent weeks getting back to their previous level—without knowing why some things started failing again.

✓ Structured

The team had the system documented: 5 pillars in one doc, a workflow in numbered boxes, and a validation checklist. In the new tool, they mapped where each pillar fit (context in the system, memory in a database, validation as a human checklist), ran the same test cases as always, and were validated and operating within a day.

Why it changes: Anyone who only had the clicks memorized was one update away from losing everything—the tool was the system. Anyone with the logic documented treated the switch as a quick reimplementation, because what matters (what needs to exist) was already written down and portable. The logic survives; the clicks don’t.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Document the system in a tool-independent format: pillars in text, workflow in boxes, validation in a checklist.
  • ✓When evaluating a new tool, ask where each pillar fits — not how to use it.
  • ✓Keep a fixed set of test cases to revalidate the system in any tool.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Memorize magic prompts tied to a specific interface.
  • ✗Treat switching tools as starting over; with documented logic, it’s a quick reimplementation.
  • ✗Confusing knowing how to click the tool with understanding the system — they are different layers.

💡Practical tip

Take the disappearance test: if your main tool disappeared tomorrow, how much of your system would survive? Write down the honest percentage. Everything you would lose is logic still trapped in the tool that needs to become a portable document.

Key concepts

The logic comes from the nature of the problem

Context, objectives, rules, memory, and validation are required by every tool and every model. They don’t belong to the software — they belong to the problem. That’s why they last.

The tool is implementation

Where to write, how to save it, which button to click—only the form changes. If you know WHAT needs to exist, finding WHERE in a new tool takes minutes.

Who understands the logic is in control

The shallow user is at the mercy of the tutorial and gets stuck when the interface changes. The architect opens the new tool and simply asks where each pillar fits.

Portable document

5 pillars in text, a flow in boxes, validation in a checklist—independent of the tool. Migrating means copying and adapting, not starting from scratch.

💎 Where this creates value

Intent Architecture isn’t whiteboard theory — it becomes something that generates revenue, saves time, or reduces risk. There are four concrete ways a system can create value, and the same 5-pillar logic applies to all four. Knowing which one you’re building keeps the project focused from day one.

Specialized assistant is a system that does one thing very well for a specific context: your store’s customer service assistant, the legal copilot that reviews contracts according to your standards, the HR assistant that screens resumes based on your criteria. Its value is depth—it knows your business, not the whole world.

Automated process is a system that runs a chain of tasks from end to end with little intervention: receive a request → generate a response → validate → record → notify. Its value is time and consistency—what used to take hours of manual work runs on its own, the same way every time, within the rules.

Digital product is a system you package and deliver to other people: an app, an internal tool that becomes external, or a service customers use. Its value is scale—the system serves many people without effort from you for each interaction. Here, validation and rules become even more critical because errors reach the end customer.

Decision system is a system that organizes information so you (or your team) can make better decisions: it analyzes scenarios, compares options against criteria, and flags risks. Its value is better decisions and reduced risk. It doesn’t decide for you—it structures the decision so it’s consistent and well-founded.

A same logic works for all four. In every case, you need context (what the AI knows), an objective (what success looks like), rules (constraints), memory (what persists), and validation (how to tell if it's correct). What changes is the packaging and who consumes the result—not the pillars.

Naming the type from the start sharpens the project. A specialized assistant prioritizes depth of context and memory; a digital product prioritizes error-proof rules and validation; an automated process prioritizes workflow and automatic validation; a decision system prioritizes objectives and comparison criteria. Knowing the type tells you where to focus your effort.

Ready-to-use example — Same problem, four forms of value

A clinic uses AI to handle patient triage and scheduling.

✗ Fragile

“I’m going to use AI to help at the clinic.” (without defining the kind of system, the project becomes a pile of unfocused prompts, with no definition of done and no one accountable for the result.)

✓ Structured

Four clear categories: (1) SPECIALIZED ASSISTANT — a copilot that answers patients’ questions according to the clinic’s standards; focus on context and memory. (2) AUTOMATED PROCESS — a workflow that confirms appointments, reschedules missed visits, and records them in the system; focus on workflow and validation. (3) DIGITAL PRODUCT — a self-service portal that other clinics subscribe to; focus on rules and error-proof validation. (4) DECISION SYSTEM — a dashboard that prioritizes the queue by urgency based on clinical criteria; focus on objectives and criteria. Each becomes a project with a defined DONE.

Why it changes: The vague scope gives you nowhere to focus your effort and no way to know whether it’s done. Naming the type sharpens the project: it defines which pillar matters most, the success criteria, and who uses the result. Same five-pillar logic, four concrete ways to deliver value.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓Name which of the four types you're building from the start.
  • ✓Focus effort on the pillar that this type requires most (context, workflow, rules, or objectives).
  • ✓Explicitly define who consumes the result and what “done” means for that consumer.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Start building without deciding on the type — it becomes a pile of unfocused prompts.
  • ✗Treat a digital product with the rigor of an internal draft; the error reaches the customer.
  • ✗Expecting a decision-making system to decide for you; it structures the decision, but does not replace it.

💡Practical tip

Before you build, complete the sentence: “I’m building a [type] that delivers [result] for [who], and it will be ready when [criterion].” If you can’t fill in all four brackets, you still don’t know what you’re building — and the project will drift.

Key concepts

Specialized assistant

Does one thing very well for a specific context. Value = depth: it knows your business, not the world. It prioritizes context and memory.

Automated process

Runs a chain of tasks from end to end with little intervention. Value = time and consistency. Prioritizes workflow and automatic validation.

Digital product

System packaged and delivered to others. Value = scale. Rules and validation become critical because errors reach the end customer.

Decision system

Organizes information to support better decisions—compares options and flags risks. Value = better decisions and less risk. Prioritizes goals and criteria.

🏁 Final project

It's time to start the engine. You'll take the 5 Pillars Canvas from day 2 and turn it into a system that actually runs: define the components, build the workflow, run it with a real case, validate it against the checklist, and write your continuous improvement plan. By the end of this topic, you won't have learned about Intent Architecture — you'll have your own working system.

Step 1 — Turn the Canvas into a System Sheet. Take the 5 pillars from day 2 and fill out the System Sheet (today’s template): components, flow, validation criteria, and improvement plan. The Canvas was the blueprint; the Sheet is the operating system.

Step 2 — Define the components. List what makes up the system: where the context lives, what’s in memory, which rules are active, what the goal is, and who uses the result. Each component needs a concrete place, not a vague intention.

Step 3 — Build the flow. Write the sequence of steps in numbered boxes: from input (the request/case) to validated output. Mark where memory is consulted, where there is autonomy (agent), and where validation happens.

Step 4 — Run it with a real case. Choose a real case from your day and run the system end to end. Write down what came out. A real case, not an ideal one — that’s the honest test.

Step 5 — Validate against the checklist. Check the result against the validation criteria item by item. Mark each one as passed or failed. Taste doesn’t count; criteria do.

Step 6 — Write the continuous improvement plan. Record the gaps you found, the cause of each, the adjustment, and the review cadence (e.g., review the log every Friday). This plan is what keeps the system alive. With the Worksheet filled out, the case run, validation done, and the plan written down, you’ve completed the setup: you have an Intention Architecture system that’s built, run, and equipped with an improvement loop.

Student wants to finish the course with a real customer service system.

✗ Fragile

✓ Structured

Completed SHEET: components defined (context in system, 10 questions in memory, 4 active rules, goal of resolving in the first response). WORKFLOW mapped in 5 boxes. RAN with 3 real customer messages. VALIDATED against 6 criteria — 2 failed (wrong timeline, escalated too much). FIXED the causes (timeline rule + refund response in memory), ran again, and passed. PLAN: review the log every Friday, with a goal of reducing escalations by 20% this month.

Why it changes: The vague Canvas has all the pieces, but the engine was never started — so no one knows if it works. Running the Worksheet proved the system with real cases, found and fixed root causes of gaps, and installed the improvement loop. One is a promise; the other is a reliable, repeatable, aligned operating system. That's what completes the course.

✓ What to DO

  • ✓You must run it with 2-3 real cases and validate each one against the checklist.
  • ✓Fix the causes of the gaps, rerun the same case, and only then call it done.
  • ✓Write the improvement plan with the cause, adjustment, and review cadence.

✗ What to AVOID

  • ✗Validating based on personal preference instead of the criteria checklist.
  • ✗Leave without an improvement plan — the system freezes at the first version.

Your real certificate isn't the PDF — it's the completed Form with at least one case run, validated, and a flaw fixed at its root cause. Keep that Form: it's your first system and the template for all the next ones. Whenever you have a new AI idea, start by filling out a Form.

Exercise 1 — Loose prompt becomes a system

Objective: Turn an improvised request into a system with all 5 pillars connected.

Take a task you currently handle with an improvised, one-off prompt (customer support, content, triage, email, summary). Rewrite it as a system: fill in the 5 pillars (Context, Objectives, Rules, Memory, Validation) and show the chain Intention → Context → Process → Result connecting them. Apply the third-party test at the end.

  1. Write the loose prompt you use today, as is.
  2. List the CONTEXT the AI needs to know that’s currently only in your head.
  3. Define the Goal (what success looks like) and 2-3 Rules (limits and prohibitions).
  4. Specify what goes into Memory and write 3 Validation criteria.
  5. Apply the third-party test: could someone else run it using only what’s written?
See the sample answer key
TASK: Reply to quote request emails for a custom woodworking shop. Loose prompt (before): Reply politely to this customer asking for a quote. SYSTEM (after): CONTEXT: Custom woodworking shop, 30-day production lead time, quote only after a site visit, serves the metropolitan area, doesn't do small repairs. CHAIN: Intent (convert lead) → Context (shop rules) → Process (qualify + propose visit) → Result (visit scheduled). THIRD-PARTY TEST: yes—a new assistant could respond according to the standard using only this document, without asking me anything.

💡 Tip: The shortcut to finding hidden Context is to list everything you would correct in a new employee’s response. Each correction is a piece of context, a rule, or an objective that lives in your head and needs to be put into writing.

Exercise 2 — Map the workflow and position the gears

Objective: Build the workflow in named steps and identify where memory, agent, and validation fit.

For the Exercise 1 system (or another case of your own), draw the flow in numbered boxes, from input to validated output. At each step, mark: does this need memory? Does this need autonomous cycles (an agent)? What rule sets the limit? Explain where you did NOT add an agent.

  1. Write the sequence of steps as numbered boxes, from input to output.
  2. Mark which steps consult memory and what it provides.
  3. Identify whether any step requires iteration (try/fail/correct)—only then does an agent make sense.
  4. Point out the rule that limits each critical step.
  5. Justify at least one step where you decided NOT to use an agent.
See the sample answer key
WORKFLOW — woodworking shop quote support: (1) RECEIVE the customer’s email. Rule: don’t respond to requests outside the service area without saying so. (2) CLASSIFY the request (quote / repair / other). Memory: list of neighborhoods and service types. (3) QUALIFY — ask the standard questions if information is missing. Memory: approved questions. Rule: don’t ask for a target price. (4) DRAFT a response proposing a visit. Memory: approved template and brand tone. AGENT here: iterate on the draft until it passes validation or, after 2 attempts, escalate to the owner — because the writing benefits from trying and correcting. (5) VALIDATE against the checklist (proposes a visit / no price / 30-day timeline / courteous tone). (6) SEND if it passes; ESCALATE to the owner if it fails 2x. WHERE I DIDN’T USE AN AGENT: step 2 (classify) — it’s a single, straightforward decision that doesn’t need iteration; cycle-based autonomy there would only add cost and another point of failure.

💡 Tip: Use the minimum rule: start with no agents and add one only where the step really requires trying, failing, and correcting without you. If the step is a one-time decision, a simple workflow is enough—an agent there adds complexity without a payoff.

Exercise 3 — Run, validate, and fix the cause

Objective: Run the run → validate → adjust → improve loop with a real case and fix the cause, not the symptom.

Run your system on a REAL case from your day. Validate the result item by item against your checklist. For each failed item, identify the CAUSE (which pillar failed) and write the structural adjustment. Then rerun the same case and confirm it passed (the same-case rule).

  1. Choose a real case (a message, a request, an actual decision).
  2. Run the system and record the result it produced.
  3. Validate against the checklist item by item: pass or fail each one.
  4. For each rejection, write down the cause (which pillar failed) and the adjustment to that pillar.
  5. Re-run the SAME case and confirm: did it pass? If it still fails, that was a symptom—adjust again.
See the sample answer key
REAL CASE: email: “I want a kitchen cabinet. How much does it cost, and when will it be ready? I live downtown.” RESULT: a friendly reply that suggested a visit, BUT said “it’ll be ready in about 3 weeks” and didn’t confirm whether downtown is in the service area. VALIDATION: - Suggests a visit: PASSED. - No price: PASSED. - Correct timing (30 days): FAILED (said 3 weeks). - Friendly tone: PASSED. - Confirms service area: FAILED (didn’t check the neighborhood). CAUSES AND ADJUSTMENTS: - Wrong timing → cause: Context didn’t specify the standard timeline → adjustment: add “production time = 30 days” to Context and “never promise a timeline shorter than 30 days” as a Rule. - Service area not confirmed → cause: a check step was missing → adjustment: add “confirm the neighborhood against the service area list” to step 2 of the flow and add “confirms service area” to the validation checklist. 💡 Tip: If the same case passes when you rerun it, but you suspect another case would break, create a small set of 3 varied real cases and run all three after every adjustment. It’s an inexpensive way to avoid replacing one gap with another.

  2. Mark each one as survives (documented/portable) or is lost (tied to the tool).
  3. Estimate the honest percentage that would survive a tool change.
  4. For each item that would be lost, write down the portable version (text, boxes, checklist).
  5. Save the result as the tool-independent master document.

🎓 To make things easier live (in-person mode)

Total time: 120 min.

Activity: “Start the Engine” workshop in trios. Opening (15 min): the facilitator shows a loose prompt failing live, then the same case as a system, connecting it to Day 1’s pyramid and Day 2’s Canvas. Block 1 — Assembly (30 min): each participant translates their Canvas into the System Worksheet (components + flow). Block 2 — Cross-Run (35 min): within the trio, each person runs the system belonging to the person on their left, using a real case that person provides, and checks it against their checklist—the system owner does NOT defend it; they only take notes on the gaps (a real-world third-party test). Block 3 — Cause-Based Adjustment (25 min): each person fixes the cause of at least one identified gap, reruns the same case, and confirms the fix. Wrap-up (15 min): each trio presents one gap that led to a structural adjustment and writes the cadence for the improvement plan. Close the course by handing in the Worksheet as a real certificate.

Materials: A3 sheets with the printed System Brief (1 per participant); pads of 2 colors of sticky notes (yellow = gap, green = adjustment); thick marker; completed day 2 Canvas (prerequisite); access to an AI tool per trio; visible timer; wall space for improvement plans.

Discussion questions:

  • Which pillar failed the most when someone else ran your system—and why was it weak?
  • Of the gaps that surfaced, how many were root causes (structure) and how many were symptoms (output)? What does that tell you about your system?
  • If the tool you used today disappeared tomorrow, how much of your Form would survive? Where is the logic still tied to the tool?
  • Which of the four types of value is your system (assistant, process, product, or decision)—and did you focus your effort on the right pillar for that type?
  • What will your actual review cadence be after the course, and what might keep you from following it? How can you guard against that?
